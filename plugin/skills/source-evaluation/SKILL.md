# Source evaluation

The highest-risk failure is a plausible but wrong citation. The next is a reflection that sounds like a summary instead of a student who learned something.

## Research and verification

- Verify authors, year, title, journal, volume, issue, pages, and DOI against the publisher's page, PubMed, or the PDF. Never assemble a citation from memory.
- A study protocol isn't the published article. Funding, grant numbers, conflict of interest statements, and outcome measures often differ between them. If you use the protocol because the article is paywalled, say so, and correct the answers once the student provides the article.
- An online-first article has a DOI but no volume, issue, or pages, and the student's PDF may be a different version. Note any mismatch.
- Search for the current edition of institutional guidance (dietary guidelines, clinical recommendations, policy reports); your knowledge may name a superseded one.
- When an institutional source is contested, say so rather than citing it as neutral, since these assignments reward that judgment, and offer a less contested alternative.
- When the student provides the article later, re-read your earlier answers against it and fix what's wrong. Expect a few discrepancies.

## What only the student can get

Library databases are behind their institution's login, so you can't get result counts or full texts there. Don't invent them. Leave a loud placeholder like `[TODO: number of results]`, and give the student the exact search string and filters to use, plus what to change if the count is implausibly high or low.

If a full text is paywalled, suggest the library's link resolver or interlibrary loan. If the student says how they got it, follow their wishes about whether to mention that.

## Reading an article

Read the limitations section first. It's the fastest route to what the paper can and can't support, and it supplies most of a trustworthiness answer. Then read the methods for sample, design, measures, and funding, and the results for the specific numbers.

## Writing

- Be specific: name the p value, sample size, instrument, and grant number. "A significant effect on <outcome> (p = <value>), with the other <N> measures non-significant" beats "the results were mixed".
- Add one observation the authors don't make, such as a design weakness they omit or a consequence they don't draw, in one sentence.
- Connect findings to the student's own situation and question, using what they told you.
- Let the conclusion deflate when that's where the evidence points ("so this isn't the lever worth pulling").

## Multi-part assignments

Parts are submitted separately and each builds on the last. Keep one consistent set of claims across them, recorded in the course notes: the question, the student's prior beliefs and where they came from, the search terms, the chosen article, and the conclusions. If a later part changes a conclusion, frame it as a revised view, and flag any tension in your summary.
